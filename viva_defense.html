1. The Asymmetric Dispatch Problem

Conventional regression optimizes $L_2$ Mean Squared Error, predicting the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y|X]$. Power grids face asymmetric economic risk:

  • Under-prediction ($y > \hat{y}$): Triggers grid frequency collapse, catastrophic blackouts, or peaker turbine dispatch ($10\times$ cost).
  • Over-prediction ($y < \hat{y}$): Triggers renewable curtailment and negative locational marginal pricing.

2. Newsvendor Critical Fractile

Balancing unit under-prediction cost ($C_{\text{under}}$) against over-prediction cost ($C_{\text{over}}$): $$\tau^* = \frac{C_{\text{under}}}{C_{\text{under}} + C_{\text{over}}}$$ With an empirical $10:1$ grid penalty ratio ($C_{\text{under}}=10, C_{\text{over}}=1$): $$\tau^* = \frac{10}{10 + 1} = \frac{10}{11} \approx 0.909 \implies \mathbf{\tau = 0.90}$$ Mathematically justifies $\tau = 0.90$ for the peak brownout margin.

3. Pinball Loss & Subgradient

Piecewise linear asymmetric objective: $$\mathcal{L}_\tau(y, \hat{y}) = (y - \hat{y}) \cdot (\tau - \mathbb{I}(y < \hat{y}))$$ First-order subgradient: $$g_i = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_\tau}{\partial \hat{y}} = \mathbb{I}(y < \hat{y}) - \tau$$ Because the true Hessian is $h_i = 0$ everywhere, LightGBM injects a constant pseudo-Hessian $h=1.0$ for Newton-Raphson leaf splitting.

4. Chernozhukov Monotone Rearrangement

Independent quantile models can suffer from quantile crossing ($\hat{q}_{0.10} > \hat{q}_{0.90}$). Sorting predicted quantiles along the quantile axis: $$\tilde{q}_\tau(x) = \inf \{y : \hat{F}^*(y) \ge \tau\}$$ is mathematically proven (Chernozhukov et al., 2010) to strictly reduce or maintain $L_p$ estimation error while enforcing $100\%$ monotonic validity.

5. Non-Parametric Hypothesis Testing

D'Agostino-Pearson ($K^2 = 12,473.86$) and Levene's test ($W = 5,958.52$) decisively reject Gaussianity and homoscedasticity. We mandate the Kruskal-Wallis $H$-test: $$H = 27,143.61 \quad (\eta_H^2 = 18.67\%)$$ proving that $18.67\%$ of total load variance is seasonal, confirmed via Bonferroni-corrected Mann-Whitney post-hoc tests (Cliff's $\delta = +0.387$).

6. Evaluation Metrics Corridor

Probabilistic forecasts are evaluated via:

  • PICP (Coverage): $\frac{1}{N}\sum \mathbb{I}(\hat{y}_{0.10} \le y_i \le \hat{y}_{0.90}) \ge 78.0\%$.
  • MPIW (Sharpness): $\frac{1}{N}\sum (\hat{y}_{0.90} - \hat{y}_{0.10})$.
  • Winkler Score: Jointly penalizes width and boundary violations with factor $\frac{2}{\alpha}$.

Why does standard Mean Squared Error ($L_2$) training fail for electrical grid dispatch, and what is the first-principles mathematical derivation that mandates selecting the nominal upper quantile $\tau = 0.90$?
A
Because electric load data follows a Pareto power-law distribution, which causes the $L_2$ gradient to explode towards infinity unless clipped to $\tau = 0.90$.
B
Under the Newsvendor critical fractile $\tau^* = \frac{C_{\text{under}}}{C_{\text{under}} + C_{\text{over}}}$, an empirical 10:1 operational penalty ratio between catastrophic blackout shedding ($C_{\text{under}}=10$) and renewable over-generation curtailment ($C_{\text{over}}=1$) strictly dictates $\tau^* = \frac{10}{11} \approx 0.909$.
C
Because LightGBM's histogram binning algorithm is mathematically constrained to evaluate quantile splits exclusively at exact decile intervals ($\tau \in \{0.1, 0.2, \dots, 0.9\}$).
D
Because the Central Limit Theorem requires an 80% confidence interval to achieve asymptotic normality over 145,000 hourly observations.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
1. The Operational Failure of $L_2$ Loss: Minimizing Mean Squared Error ($\frac{1}{N}\sum(y - \hat{y})^2$) estimates the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y|X]$. In power systems engineering, an error of $+1,000\text{ MW}$ (over-generation) does not carry the same economic or societal cost as $-1,000\text{ MW}$ (grid collapse). Under-predicting peak demand collapses grid frequency, tripping safety breakers and causing cascading blackouts or forced peaker-turbine firing ($10\times$ cost).
Mathematical Whiteboard Derivation: Newsvendor Critical Fractile
Let $C_{\text{under}}$ be the cost per unit of under-prediction ($y > \hat{y}$), and $C_{\text{over}}$ be the cost per unit of over-prediction ($y < \hat{y}$). The expected operational dispatch cost is: $$E[C(\hat{y})] = C_{\text{over}} \int_{-\infty}^{\hat{y}} (\hat{y} - y) f(y) dy + C_{\text{under}} \int_{\hat{y}}^{\infty} (y - \hat{y}) f(y) dy$$ Differentiating with respect to $\hat{y}$ using Leibniz's rule: $$\frac{d}{d\hat{y}} E[C(\hat{y})] = C_{\text{over}} F(\hat{y}) - C_{\text{under}} (1 - F(\hat{y})) = 0$$ $$F(\hat{y}^*) (C_{\text{over}} + C_{\text{under}}) = C_{\text{under}} \implies F(\hat{y}^*) = \tau^* = \frac{C_{\text{under}}}{C_{\text{under}} + C_{\text{over}}}$$ With an empirical $10:1$ operational penalty ratio ($C_{\text{under}} = 10, C_{\text{over}} = 1$): $$\tau^* = \frac{10}{10 + 1} = \frac{10}{11} \approx 0.909 \implies \mathbf{\tau = 0.90}$$
Viva Defense Soundbite
"We optimize Pinball loss at $\tau=0.90$ because an asymmetric $10:1$ cost structure between spinning reserve dispatch and curtailment mathematically maps to the 90.9th percentile under the Newsvendor theorem. An $L_2$ mean regressor would under-dispatch reserves in 50% of peak hours."

Gradient Boosting Mechanics & Non-Differentiable Objectives
What is the exact mathematical gradient of the Pinball Loss in LightGBM, and how does the engine perform Newton-Raphson tree splitting when the second derivative (Hessian) is zero everywhere?
A
The gradient is $2(y - \hat{y})$, and LightGBM applies an exact second-order Taylor expansion using numerical finite differences.
B
The subgradient is $g_i = \mathbb{I}(y_i < \hat{y}_i) - \tau$. Because the true Hessian is zero almost everywhere ($h_i = 0$), LightGBM substitutes a constant pseudo-Hessian $h_i = 1.0$ (or Huber-approximated smoothing) to prevent division by zero in leaf weight updates.
C
LightGBM switches from histogram-based splitting to Nelder-Mead simplex search across non-differentiable leaf boundaries.
D
The Hessian is given by $\frac{\tau(1-\tau)}{\hat{y}^2}$, providing an adaptive learning rate during training.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
1. Pinball Loss Formulation: $$\mathcal{L}_\tau(y, \hat{y}) = (y - \hat{y}) \cdot \Big(\tau - \mathbb{I}(y < \hat{y})\Big)$$ 2. First-Order Subgradient: $$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_\tau}{\partial \hat{y}} = \mathbb{I}(y < \hat{y}) - \tau = \begin{cases} -\tau & \text{if } y > \hat{y} \text{ (under-prediction)} \\ 1 - \tau & \text{if } y < \hat{y} \text{ (over-prediction)} \end{cases}$$
The Zero-Hessian Dilemma in Newton-Raphson Boosting
In standard XGBoost / LightGBM, the optimal leaf weight for leaf $j$ is calculated via: $$w_j^* = -\frac{\sum_{i \in I_j} g_i}{\sum_{i \in I_j} h_i + \lambda}$$ Because the piecewise linear pinball loss has a second derivative of $h_i = \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}_\tau}{\partial \hat{y}^2} = 0$ everywhere (and undefined at 0), standard Newton-Raphson division fails. LightGBM resolves this by substituting a constant pseudo-Hessian ($h_i = 1.0$) or internally applying a Huber-quantile approximation, reducing the leaf update to a regularized subgradient step.

Temporal Leakage Invariants & Quantile Crossing

A
K-Fold CV causes memory fragmentation in pandas; quantile crossing is resolved by retraining on log-transformed load values.
B
K-Fold allows future timestamps ($t+1$) to leak into past predictions ($t$) over autocorrelated features ($r > 0.95$); quantile crossing is resolved via Chernozhukov Monotone Rearrangement, which sorts predicted quantiles per sample.
C
K-Fold destroys seasonal frequency harmonics; quantile crossing is prevented by clipping negative outputs to zero.
D
K-Fold is forbidden by FERC regulatory rules; quantile crossing is prevented by using a single neural network with softmax outputs.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
1. The Temporal Leakage Trap: Hourly power load exhibits massive autocorrelation ($\text{ACF}(1) = 0.982$, $\text{ACF}(24) = 0.891$). Random K-Fold splits observations randomly, meaning the model is trained on hour $t+1$ and evaluated on hour $t$. With autoregressive features like lag_1, random K-Fold achieves an artificially deflated test error that collapses in real-world deployment. We mandate expanding-window TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5).
Mathematical Proof: Chernozhukov Monotone Rearrangement (2010)
When independent regressors $\hat{q}_{\tau}(x)$ are trained for $\tau \in \{0.10, 0.50, 0.90\}$, sample estimation error can cause the Quantile Crossing Anomaly: $$\hat{q}_{0.10}(x) > \hat{q}_{0.90}(x)$$ Let $\hat{F}^*(y) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M \mathbb{I}(\hat{q}_{\tau_m}(x) \le y)$. The monotone rearranged quantile is defined as the generalized inverse: $$\tilde{q}_\tau(x) = \inf \{y : \hat{F}^*(y) \ge \tau\}$$ Chernozhukov et al. proved that sorting the predicted quantiles $[\hat{q}_{0.10}, \hat{q}_{0.50}, \hat{q}_{0.90}]$ monotonically per observation strictly reduces or maintains estimation error under any $L_p$ norm ($p \ge 1$): $$\|\tilde{q}_\tau(x) - q_\tau(x)\|_p \le \|\hat{q}_\tau(x) - q_\tau(x)\|_p$$ This guarantees $0.00\%$ crossing rate without retraining.

Statistical Diagnostics & Omnibus Gatekeeping
Why did your statistical pipeline reject standard ANOVA in favor of the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test across the four seasons, and what was the observed effect size ($\eta_H^2$)?
A
D'Agostino-Pearson ($K^2 = 12,473.86, p < 10^{-300}$) and Levene's test ($W = 5,958.52, p < 10^{-300}$) rejected normality and homoscedasticity; Kruskal-Wallis established $H = 27,143.61$ with seasonal effect size $\eta_H^2 = 18.67\%$.
B
ANOVA cannot compute degrees of freedom when sample sizes exceed 100,000 rows; the Kruskal-Wallis test reduced variance by 99.4%.
C
ANOVA requires categorical variables to be strictly binary; the Kruskal-Wallis test was chosen to enable Bonferroni p-value amplification.
D
The data exhibited Gaussian residuals with equal variances, but Kruskal-Wallis was selected because of its higher statistical power.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
1. Chapter 2 Statistical Discipline (Assumption Validation): One-way ANOVA relies strictly on two foundational assumptions:
  • Normality of group populations: Tested via D'Agostino-Pearson $K^2 = s^2 + k^2 = 12,473.86$ ($p < 10^{-300}$, rejecting $H_0$).
  • Homoscedasticity (equal variance across groups): Tested via Levene's test $W = 5,958.52$ ($p < 10^{-300}$, severe heteroscedasticity).
Non-Parametric Omnibus Gatekeeper & Eta-Squared Effect Size
Because parametric assumptions were violated, running ANOVA would produce inflated Type I errors. We executed the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test: $$H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)} \sum_{j=1}^k \frac{R_j^2}{n_j} - 3(N+1) = 27,143.61 \quad (p < 10^{-300})$$ We report the non-parametric effect size $\eta_H^2$: $$\eta_H^2 = \frac{H - k + 1}{N - k} = \frac{27,143.61 - 4 + 1}{145,392 - 4} \approx 0.1867 \implies \mathbf{18.67\%}$$ Conclusion: $18.67\%$ of the total variance in electricity demand ranks is attributable strictly to seasonal shift, followed by post-hoc Bonferroni-corrected Mann-Whitney $U$ tests (Summer vs. Spring Cliff's $\delta = +0.387$).
Question 5
Model Interpretability & TreeSHAP Attribution Axioms
Why are split-count feature importances untrustworthy for grid dispatch explanations, and what mathematical axioms does TreeSHAP guarantee?
A
Split counts are biased toward continuous, high-cardinality features and violate consistency; TreeSHAP satisfies Local Accuracy (Additivity: $\sum \phi_i = f(x) - E[f]$), Missingness, and Monotonic Consistency in polynomial time $\mathcal{O}(TLD^2)$.
B
Split counts cannot be computed on boosted trees; TreeSHAP uses backpropagation to compute Hessian derivatives of each tree leaf.
C
Split counts require GPU CUDA acceleration; TreeSHAP is an unsupervised clustering algorithm that isolates anomaly timestamps.
D
TreeSHAP sets all negative feature attributions to zero, ensuring only positive load drivers are presented to grid dispatchers.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
The Fatal Flaw of Split-Count Feature Importance: Standard Gini or split-frequency importance measures how often a feature is chosen for a split. Continuous features with infinite possible split thresholds (like temperature or rolling load) artificially inflate split counts over binary features (like is_holiday), even when the binary feature exerts a massive structural impact. Furthermore, split importance violates Consistency: altering a model so that a feature has a larger marginal impact can paradoxically lower its split importance!
TreeSHAP Axioms (Lundberg et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2020)
TreeSHAP calculates exact Shapley values from cooperative game theory: $$\phi_i(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} \Big( f_x(S \cup \{i\}) - f_x(S) \Big)$$ It guarantees three vital properties:
  1. Local Accuracy (Additivity): The sum of all feature attributions equals the difference between the sample prediction and the baseline expected value: $$\sum_{i=1}^M \phi_i(x) = f(x) - \mathbb{E}[f(X)]$$
  2. Missingness: Features that do not appear in a tree path receive $\phi_i = 0$.
  3. Consistency: If a model changes such that the marginal contribution of feature $i$ increases or stays the same, $\phi_i$ never decreases.
In our summer peak waterfall analysis ($49,566\text{ MW}$), TreeSHAP proved that rolling_max_24 ($+5,240\text{ MW}$) and lag_1 ($+4,120\text{ MW}$) were the primary drivers elevating the baseline from $32,150\text{ MW}$ up to $48,200\text{ MW}$.

A
PICP measures the empirical percentage of actual demand falling inside $[\hat{y}_{0.10}, \hat{y}_{0.90}]$; MPIW measures interval sharpness (width in MW); the Winkler score rewards narrow intervals while heavily penalizing boundary violations by $\frac{2}{\alpha} \times \text{distance}$.

Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
Whiteboard Formulation: PICP, MPIW & Winkler Score
Given an 80% nominal prediction interval corridor $[L_i, U_i] = [\hat{y}_{0.10}^{(i)}, \hat{y}_{0.90}^{(i)}]$:

1. Prediction Interval Coverage Probability (PICP): $$\text{PICP} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(L_i \le y_i \le U_i)$$ Measures the empirical reliability of the safety corridor. For nominal $(1 - \alpha) = 0.80$, acceptance requires $\text{PICP} \ge 78.0\%$.

2. Mean Prediction Interval Width (MPIW - Sharpness): $$\text{MPIW} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (U_i - L_i)$$ A trivially infinite interval achieves $100\%$ PICP but is useless for grid scheduling. Forecasting excellence minimizes MPIW subject to $\text{PICP} \ge 1 - \alpha$.

3. Winkler Score ($W_\alpha$): $$W_\alpha(y_i, L_i, U_i) = (U_i - L_i) + \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } L_i \le y_i \le U_i \\ \frac{2}{\alpha}(L_i - y_i) & \text{if } y_i < L_i \\ \frac{2}{\alpha}(y_i - U_i) & \text{if } y_i > U_i \end{cases}$$ For $\alpha = 0.20$, $\frac{2}{\alpha} = 10.0$. Observations escaping the safety corridor are penalized $10\times$ their violation distance!

Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
The Tree Shrinkage Bias in Quantile Boosting: Gradient boosting algorithms enforce regularization via learning rate shrinkage ($\eta = 0.04$), minimum data per leaf (min_child_samples=50), and $L_2$ leaf penalties. In extreme quantiles ($\tau = 0.10$ and $\tau = 0.90$), sample density in tree leaves is sparse (only 10% of samples lie in the target tail). Regularization pulls these extreme leaf estimates inward toward the sample median $\mathbb{E}[Y]$. This shrinkage makes the predicted interval $[q_{10}, q_{90}]$ slightly too narrow, resulting in empirical test coverage of $\approx 76.2\%$ instead of the nominal $80\%$.
Remediation Procedure R-1 (Boundary Offset Tuning)

  • Upper tail exceedance ($y > \hat{q}_{0.90}$): $11.8\%$ (target: $10\%$).
  • Lower tail exceedance ($y < \hat{q}_{0.10}$): $12.0\%$ (target: $10\%$).
We trained calibrated boundary regressors at nominal offsets $\tau_{\text{lower}} = 0.08$ and $\tau_{\text{upper}} = 0.92$. This mathematically compensates for leaf shrinkage, expanding the boundary corridor to yield $\text{PICP} = \mathbf{78.8\%}$ out-of-sample while preserving sharp median accuracy.

Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
Target Leakage Mechanics in Rolling Windows
In pandas, a rolling window of size $W$ at index $t$ spans: $$\text{rolling}(W)_t = [x_{t - W + 1}, \; \dots, \; x_{t-1}, \; \mathbf{x_t}]$$ If applied directly to the target variable `PJME_MW`, the rolling window at hour $t$ includes the actual realization $y_t$ in its mean and maximum!

During tree construction, LightGBM would discover that $y_t$ is contained within `rolling_max_24` and `rolling_mean_24`. The model would learn a trivial identity mapping, showing near-zero training error but failing completely during live inference when $y_t$ is unknown.

The Invariant: In [`src/feature_engineering.py`](file:///c:/Users/aksha/DSAI_Projects/Project1_Energy_Grid_Forecasting/src/feature_engineering.py#L45-L50):
shifted_series = df_out[TARGET_COL].shift(1)
df_out[f"rolling_mean_{window}"] = shifted_series.rolling(window=window).mean()
This guarantees that all rolling statistics are computed strictly over $\{y_{t-W}, \dots, y_{t-1}\}$, preserving temporal causality.

Temporal Representations & Cyclical Trigonometric Encodings
Why are raw integer hours (0 to 23) augmented with cyclical trigonometric encodings ($\sin(2\pi h/24)$ and $\cos(2\pi h/24)$)?
A
Linear integer hours introduce an artificial discontinuity between 23:00 and 00:00 (distance = 23); sine and cosine projections map time onto a continuous 2D unit circle where Euclidean distance is continuous ($\Delta = 1$).
B
LightGBM decision trees cannot split on integer features unless they are converted into floating-point numbers.
C
Trigonometric encodings are required by the fast Fourier transform (FFT) during TreeSHAP evaluation.
D
To prevent multicollinearity between day of week and month of year.
Examiner Solution & First-Principles Defense Verified
The Boundary Discontinuity Problem
Time is fundamentally periodic. Hour 23:00 (11 PM) and Hour 00:00 (Midnight) are separated by exactly 1 hour in reality. However, when represented as raw integers: $$|23 - 0| = 23 \quad \text{(Maximum possible distance!)}$$ A decision tree splitting on $h \ge 20$ isolates late night, but cannot naturally group 23:00 and 01:00 together without creating two disconnected leaves.

Cyclical Unit Circle Projection: $$x_{\text{sin}} = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot h}{24}\right), \quad x_{\text{cos}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi \cdot h}{24}\right)$$ The Euclidean distance between 23:00 ($h=23$) and 00:00 ($h=0$): $$\Delta = \sqrt{(\sin(23\cdot 2\pi/24) - \sin(0))^2 + (\cos(23\cdot 2\pi/24) - \cos(0))^2} \approx 0.261$$ Preserves geometric continuity across midnight, smoothing diurnal ramp transitions.
